Game Theory-Based Analysis of Strategic Decision-Making in Cloud Resource Allocation

Two cloud tenants share a finite pool of CPU, memory and accelerators. Each chooses how much capacity to claim. This site models that choice as a normal-form game and lets you compute its equilibria, welfare loss and sensitivity yourself.

Research question. Do individually rational cloud resource allocation choices lead to an outcome that is stable and collectively efficient?
Explore the Model / Open Game SimulatorView Results

Three strategies

  • O — Over-provision: hold more capacity than needed.
  • R — Right-size: request only what the workload needs.
  • V — Reserved: commit to dedicated capacity at a fixed price.

B · PROBLEMShared capacity, private incentives

The shared-capacity problem

CPU, memory and accelerator capacity are finite. When tenants compete, demand above capacity causes congestion and queueing, hurting latency and service-level objectives (SLOs). Capacity held but unused creates idle-capacity cost. Each tenant’s best private move can differ from what is best for everyone together: individual incentives versus collective welfare.

Strategies in detail

Over-provision (O). Holds more than the workload needs. Protects short-term performance, but congests the pool when many tenants do the same.

Right-size (R). Requests only what is needed, relying on autoscaling and pooling. Efficient, but exposed when rivals hoard capacity.

Reserved (V). Fixed-price dedicated capacity. Reduces exposure to shared-pool contention but costs money when idle.

Payoffs are stylized utility units (a blend of throughput, latency/SLO attainment and cost). They are not calibrated against AWS, Azure, Google Cloud or Kubernetes measurements.

C · OBJECTIVES & METHODWhat the project does

  1. Model two competing tenants as a normal-form game.
  2. Identify best responses, dominant strategies, pure and mixed Nash equilibria, and Pareto-efficient outcomes.
  3. Measure equilibrium inefficiency with the Price of Anarchy.
  4. Compare the baseline game, Stag Hunt, Hawk-Dove and the extended game with Reserved.
  5. Study how platform efficiency credits change incentives.
  6. Explain repeated interaction and the Folk Theorem as a theoretical extension.
  7. Explain the reproducible Python implementation and its outputs.

Normal-form game

G = (N, S, u)

I · SENSITIVITYEfficiency-credit simulator

A platform credit b is added to the payoff of every tenant that chooses Right-size (to that tenant’s own payoff only). Baseline cells become (O,O)=(3,3), (O,R)=(8,2+b), (R,O)=(2+b,8), (R,R)=(6+b,6+b).

Limitation: welfare counts credits received by tenants but does not subtract the platform’s cost of funding them.

Why a small credit can backfire: below b = 1, Over-provision stays strictly dominant, so behavior (and equilibrium welfare of 6) does not change, yet the attainable maximum rises (12 → 13 at b = 0.5). The ratio therefore grows, from 2.00 to about 2.17. At the boundaries b = 1 and b = 2, payoff ties create multiple best responses; the engine reports all of them.

J · THEORETICAL EXTENSIONRepeated games and the Folk Theorem

Theoretical illustration only. The report’s Python program analyses the one-shot game and does not simulate repeated play.

In an indefinitely repeated game with discount factor δ, tenants can sustain mutual Right-sizing (cooperation) using Tit-for-Tat. Temptation payoff 8, cooperation payoff 6, punishment payoff 3.

Cooperate forever: 6 / (1 − δ)
Defect once, then punished: 8 + 3δ / (1 − δ)
Sustainable when 6 ≥ 8 − 5δ ⇒ δ ≥ (8 − 6)/(8 − 3) = 0.40

M · ALGORITHMSDefinitions

Best response

For each opponent strategy, keep every own strategy that maximizes own payoff (ties kept).

Strict dominance

Strategy s is strictly dominant if it pays strictly more than every alternative against every opponent strategy.

Pure Nash search

for each cell (i,j):
  if i in BR_A(j) and j in BR_B(i):
      report (i,j)

For an n×n game this is O(n²) work after best responses are tabulated. That claim is for this search only.

Mixed equilibrium (2×2)

q = (a22 − a12)/(a11 − a12 − a21 + a22)

q = probability B plays the first strategy, from A’s indifference. p for A comes from B’s payoffs the same way. Valid only if the denominator is nonzero and 0 < probability < 1.

Pareto, welfare, PoA

Pareto filter: drop an outcome if another is ≥ for both and > for one. Social optimum maximizes the payoff sum. PoA = max welfare / worst Nash welfare (undefined if there is no equilibrium or worst welfare ≤ 0).

N · APPLICATIONSWhere the model applies

1 · Platform pricing

Credits, congestion surcharges and quotas reshape payoffs. See the credit simulator: crossing b > 2 flips the equilibrium to (R,R).

2 · Tenant strategy

Reserved capacity shields a tenant from a congested pool. In the 3×3 game, PoA drops from 2.00 to 1.20.

3 · Scheduler policy

PoA quantifies how much welfare allocation incentives destroy, a yardstick for comparing policies.

4 · Teaching

One concrete cloud example illustrates dominance, equilibrium, coordination and anti-coordination.

O · LIMITS & FUTUREScope

Limitations (what exists today)

  • Payoffs are stylized, not estimated from real cluster traces or price lists.
  • Only two players.
  • The program analyses the one-shot game.
  • Repeated interaction is theoretical.
  • Credit welfare omits the platform’s funding cost.

Future scope (proposed, not built)

  • Estimate payoffs from real usage data.
  • Simulate repeated play with learning agents.
  • Study N-player and network games.
  • Explore fair allocation such as Dominant Resource Fairness.
  • Model the platform as a third player that sets allocation rules.
